1. Executive Summary

This proposal presents Crisis Cabinet: IT Project Risk Simulation (COM0463), a Digital Scenario-Based Learning Game targeting COM0463 IT Project Management students (3rd/4th year undergraduates, n≈40) at Istanbul Kültür University, Department of Computer Engineering.

Students in COM0463 can recite risk management frameworks (PMBOK §11, Tusler classification) in exams, but consistently fail to apply them under pressure in real project contexts. Conventional case studies lack urgency and consequence. Crisis Cabinet places each student in the role of a project manager facing live IT crises — they must classify each risk using the Tusler probability × impact matrix, select the correct response strategy (avoid/mitigate/transfer/accept), and specify concrete mitigation actions within a countdown timer.

The project is grounded in Bloom's Taxonomy [Bloom et al., 1956], MDA Framework [Hunicke et al., 2004], Self-Determination Theory [Deci & Ryan, 1985], and Flow Theory [Csikszentmihalyi, 1990], employing Points, Progress Tracking, Challenge, Timer, Badges, and Feedback as primary mechanics. The programme spans 3 sprints (March–May 2026) and targets a Tusler classification accuracy improvement from 42% (baseline) to ≥70% post-session.

Game Overview: Single HTML file, zero server dependency, 45-minute session, 12 IT project crisis scenarios across 4 PMBOK phases. Each crisis requires 3 sequential decisions: (1) Tusler classification → (2) PMBOK response strategy → (3) specific mitigation action. Timer pressure, health bar, streak bonus, and live GPPT dimension bars.

Conventional case studies lack urgency and consequence. 7. Six-Dimension Design

What — Game Content

12 IT project crisis scenarios mapped to 4 PMBOK phases (Planning S1–S3, Executing S4–S6, Monitoring S7–S9, Closing S10–S12). Each crisis requires three sequential decisions: (1) Tusler classification — Tiger (High Prob × High Impact), Alligator (Low × High), Puppy (High × Low), Kitten (Low × Low); (2) PMBOK response strategy — Avoid / Mitigate / Transfer / Accept; (3) specific mitigation action — one correct (D7-compliant: specific, time-bounded, deliverable-focused), three distractors (vague, disproportionate, or generic). Countdown timer 45s per decision. Project Health bar (100% start). Streak multiplier ×1.5 at ≥3 consecutive correct. Five GPPT dimension bars tracked live (D1 Pedagogical Alignment, D2 Game-Learning Integration, D3 Motivational Design, D7 Risk Management, D8 Innovation & Quality).

Why — Pedagogical Rationale

Flow (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990): difficulty escalates from Planning through Executing to Monitoring — avoiding boredom at entry and anxiety at peak. SDT Competence (Deci & Ryan, 1985): three-step decision chain progressively builds from recognition (classify) to strategy selection to specific action formulation. MDA (Hunicke et al., 2004): Mechanics = crisis card + timer + health; Dynamics = loss-aversion pressure from health bar + momentum from streak; Aesthetics = Challenge + Discovery + Accomplishment. Bloom L3–L5 activated across the three decision types.

Who — Player Profiles

Achievers 40%: score maximisation, streak bonuses, grade ladder A+→D. Explorers 25%: discovering why each strategy is optimal through detailed PMBOK-grounded feedback. Free Spirits 20%: replaying scenarios at own pace to master all four Tusler categories. Killers 15%: minimising health damage, achieving perfect runs with zero timeouts. All four types served without forcing competitive modes on non-competitive players.

When — Session Timing

Single in-class session, Week 8–9 of COM0463 (Chapter 8 Risk Management). 45 minutes total: 5-min facilitator briefing → 5-min Tusler grid walkthrough → 30-min gameplay → 5-min results debrief. Can be replayed independently for revision before Chapter 8 assessment.

How — Delivery Method

Single HTML file deployed via LMS download link or USB drive. No install, no login, no internet required after initial font load. Facilitator displays briefing screen on projector; students play individually on laptops or university lab machines. Post-game: instructor screenshots Results screens for cohort Tusler accuracy analysis.

Where — Technology

Vanilla JS + CSS custom properties. Single-file HTML. Fonts (Space Mono, Syne) from Google Fonts with monospace/sans-serif graceful fallback. Compatible with COM0463 university computer lab environment (Chrome ≥110, Firefox ≥110). Offline-capable after first load.

8. Concept-to-Game Mapping

PM ConceptGame MechanicDynamicAestheticRationale
Tusler risk classification (probability × impact 2×2 matrix) Four-option classify step per crisis card. Crisis data panel shows three explicit metrics: Probability (%), Impact (severity label), Urgency (label) to force matrix reading rather than intuition. Student reads crisis situation text + data panel → maps values to Tusler quadrant → selects category → immediate correct/wrong feedback with full explanation of the probability × impact logic. Challenge + Discovery The most common Chapter 8 exam failure is applying Tusler labels without reading both probability AND impact together. The data panel forces the student to use the matrix explicitly.
PMBOK §11 response strategies (Avoid / Mitigate / Transfer / Accept) Four-option strategy selection step. Options are scenario-specific PM actions (not generic one-word labels) — e.g. 'Mitigate — Require the departing developer to deliver complete schema documentation and conduct 4 knowledge-transfer sessions before their last day'. After Tusler classification, student selects strategy. Wrong selections reveal why that strategy is sub-optimal for this Tusler category and scenario context. Correct strategy is logged to the Strategy Tracker sidebar. Strategy + Challenge Students confuse Mitigate with Transfer and conflate Accept (monitored, with reserves) with passive neglect. Scenario-specific option labels force reasoning about PM context.
Mitigation specificity (GPPT D7 criterion) Four-option mitigation step. Correct option is always the most specific, time-bounded, deliverable-focused action. Distractors: vague monitoring statements, budget-only responses, process-only responses. Selecting a vague mitigation triggers a penalty and feedback: 'Generic mitigations score zero on the D7 rubric — GPPT D7 requires actions that are specific, time-bounded, and deliverable-focused.' Accomplishment + Discovery The D7 rubric penalises generic risk register entries. The game trains the specific vocabulary and structure of a professional mitigation action before students write their own risk registers.
Risk monitoring and control — consequence of inaction (PMBOK §11.7) Project Health bar decreases on wrong answers and timeouts. Tiger/Alligator errors cause 10–15 HP damage; Puppy/Kitten errors cause 5 HP damage. Health reaching 0 = project failure. Differential damage rate replicates that high-impact risks unmanaged cause proportionally more project damage. Students who consistently misclassify Tigers as Kittens see their health drain rapidly. Sensation + Challenge Students understand risk monitoring conceptually but do not feel its consequences. The health bar makes the differential cost of mismanaging high-severity risks immediately felt.
Risk monitoring as a continuous process (PMBOK §11.7) Streak bonus multiplier: ×1.5 points awarded when the player answers 3+ consecutive decision steps correctly across scenarios. Streak resets to 0 on any wrong answer or timeout. Sustained correct decision-making across multiple scenarios is rewarded compoundingly — mirroring the PMBOK principle that continuous risk vigilance across a project lifecycle yields compounding protective benefit. Accomplishment + Challenge PMBOK §11.7 emphasises that risk monitoring is not a one-time planning activity but a sustained process. The streak mechanic operationalises this behaviourally: continuous vigilance rewarded, monitoring lapses penalised.

S5 — Daily Standup Overruns Puppy 🐶 Executing

S7 — CPI 0.77 Budget Overrun Tiger 🐯 Monitoring

Setup: Monitoring phase. CPI 0.77 — project is spending 1.30 for every 1.00 of work delivered. Confirmed overrun (probability = 1.0). High impact (project viability at risk). Tiger — immediate, active response required.

Max Points: 80 | Difficulty: High

S8 — PDF Rendering Border Issue Kitten 🐱 Monitoring

Setup: Monitoring phase. Confirmed cosmetic issue: export PDF shows a 1px border artefact on certain printers. Affects ~3% of print jobs. No functional impact. Probability CONFIRMED (1.0) × Impact NEGLIGIBLE (cosmetic only).

Correct Strategy: Accept — not Mitigate. This is the key proportionality trap. Allocating sprint time to fix a 3% cosmetic issue is disproportionate.

Common Error: Students over-mitigate Kitten risks. This scenario specifically tests risk proportionality judgment. Contrast with S12 where Mitigate is correct.

Max Points: 80 | Difficulty: High (trap scenario)

S9 — Single-AZ Cloud Failure Alligator 🐊 Monitoring

Tusler Category: Alligator — Low probability (AWS AZ failure is rare: ~0.1% per year), High impact (complete service outage if triggered). Second most contested scenario.

Max Points: 80 | Difficulty: High

S10 — UAT Sign-Off Delays Puppy 🐶 Closing

Tusler Category: Puppy — High probability (UAT delays are common), Low impact (buffer exists; acceptance criteria are clear). Mitigate with structured sign-off escalation.

Max Points: 80 | Difficulty: Standard

S11 — Performance SLA Breach at Load Test Tiger 🐯 Closing

Setup: Closing phase. Final load test reveals response time 3.2s at 1,000 concurrent users — SLA requires ≤2s. Deployment deadline in 4 days. Probability CONFIRMED × Impact HIGH (contract penalty + go-live delay). Tiger.

Max Points: 80 | Difficulty: High

S12 — Broken Documentation Link Kitten 🐱 Closing

Setup: Closing phase. One internal wiki link in the handover documentation points to a deprecated page. Probability CONFIRMED (link is broken) × Impact NEGLIGIBLE (documentation only, feature works). Kitten.

Correct Strategy: Mitigate — unlike S8 (Accept), a 15-minute fix in a 9-day window means Mitigate is proportionate. This tests whether students understand that Kitten does not always mean Accept — it means proportionate response.

Max Points: 80 | Difficulty: Standard
